<div class="pair opener">
  <div class="prose">
    <p>About 2,400 years ago the Greek physician Hippocrates looked at a hard tumor with swollen veins reaching out from it and thought of a crab, <i>karkinos</i>. The Latin word for crab is <i>cancer</i>. The name stuck, and so did the picture of something that grabs hold of the body and spreads.</p>
    <p>The crab is a memorable image, but it hides the most important fact about the disease. <strong>Nothing crawls into you from outside.</strong> Cancer is made of your own cells, ones that have stopped following the rules every other cell obeys. This chapter explains what those rules are, how a cell comes to break them, and what happens next.</p>

<section class="ch" id="city">
  <div class="ch-head"><span class="ch-num">Chapter 1</span><h2>A city of 37 trillion cells</h2></div>
  <div class="pair">
    <div class="prose">
      <p>Your body is built from roughly 30 to 37 trillion <em class="term">cells</em>. Picture them as citizens of a huge, well-run city. Skin cells are the walls, red blood cells are the delivery trucks, nerve cells run the phone lines. No cell works alone, and every one of them follows the same four rules:</p>
      <ul class="rules">
        <li><span class="ic">1</span><div><strong>Divide only when told.</strong> A cell copies itself only when its neighbours send a chemical "grow" signal, for example to heal a cut.</div></li>
        <li><span class="ic">2</span><div><strong>Stop when told.</strong> When the job is done, "stop" signals switch division off again.</div></li>
        <li><span class="ic">3</span><div><strong>Fix your mistakes.</strong> Every cell carries repair kits that check its DNA and correct errors.</div></li>
        <li><span class="ic">4</span><div><strong>Die when it's time.</strong> Old or badly damaged cells self-destruct in a tidy, planned way called <em class="term">apoptosis</em>, and their parts are recycled.</div></li>
      </ul>
      <p>When a cell does divide, it runs through a fixed sequence called the <em class="term">cell cycle</em>: grow, copy the DNA, check the copy, split in two. Along the way are checkpoints where the cell stops and inspects itself, like quality inspectors on a factory line.</p>
    </div>
    <aside class="note calm">
      <b>Constant renewal</b>
      Your body replaces about 330 billion cells every day, mostly blood cells and the lining of the gut. That works out to nearly 4 million new cells every second. Every one of those divisions is a chance for something to go wrong.
    </aside>
  </div>

<!-- ============ CHAPTER 2 ============ -->
<section class="ch" id="rulebreaker">
  <div class="ch-head"><span class="ch-num">Chapter 2</span><h2>When a cell breaks the rules</h2></div>
  <div class="pair">
    <div class="prose">
      <p><strong>Cancer is what happens when one cell stops obeying the four rules and passes that disobedience on to every cell it produces.</strong> It divides without being told, ignores stop signals, skips its repairs and refuses to die. Over years its descendants pile up into a lump called a <em class="term">tumor</em>. In the worst case they push into nearby tissue and travel to other organs.</p>
      <p>This is why cancer is not one disease. Breast cancer, leukemia and skin cancer behave very differently because they start in different kinds of cells and break different rules in different ways. Doctors recognise more than a hundred types. What they share is the same basic story: a cell's own controls fail.</p>
    </div>
    <aside class="note">
      <b>Cancer cells are "immortal"</b>
      Normal cells can divide only around 50 times before they retire. Many cancer cells switch on an enzyme, telomerase, that resets this counter. A line of cervical cancer cells taken from Henrietta Lacks in 1951, called HeLa, is still dividing in labs today.
    </aside>
  </div>

  <div class="pair">
    <div class="prose">
      <p>This is the question everyone asks, and the answer surprises most people: <strong>cancer does not get into the body. It is built inside it,</strong> out of your own cells, when the DNA manual gets changed.</p>

<!-- ============ CHAPTER 5 ============ -->
<section class="ch" id="hits">
  <div class="ch-head"><span class="ch-num">Chapter 5</span><h2>One mistake is not enough</h2></div>
  <div class="pair">
    <div class="prose">
      <p>A single mutation almost never causes cancer. A cell usually needs several "hits" in different genes: an accelerator stuck, a brake or two broken, the mechanics gone, the self-destruct switch disabled. Each hit gives the cell a small advantage, so its descendants slowly outnumber their neighbours, and one of those descendants picks up the next hit.</p>
      <p>This takes time. Going from the first hit to a dangerous cancer often takes 10 to 20 years or more. It is also why cancer is mainly a disease of later life: the longer you live, the more divisions your cells have made, and the more chances for hits to collect in one unlucky cell line.</p>
    </div>
    <aside class="note calm">
      <b>Your bodyguards</b>
      Most cells with dangerous mutations never become cancer. Repair genes fix the damage, the p53 protein orders self-destruct, and immune cells (T cells and natural killer cells) hunt down cells that look abnormal. A cancer is a cell that slipped past all three.
    </aside>
  </div>

<section class="ch" id="review">
  <div class="ch-head"><span class="ch-num">Review</span><h2>The chapter in one breath</h2></div>
  <div class="prose">
    <p>Your cells follow rules written in DNA. Typos in that DNA come from copying errors, inheritance and outside damage. When enough typos hit the accelerator, brake and repair genes of one cell, it divides without limit, its descendants form a tumor, and if they learn to invade and travel, that tumor becomes a cancer that can spread.</p>
  </div>

  <h3 class="sub-h">Key terms</h3>
  <dl class="gloss">
    <div><dt>Mutation</dt><dd>A permanent change in the DNA letters of a cell.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Oncogene</dt><dd>A mutated "accelerator" gene that is stuck on and drives constant division.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Tumor suppressor gene</dt><dd>A "brake" gene; cancer often needs both copies broken.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Apoptosis</dt><dd>Planned cell self-destruct. Cancer cells learn to dodge it.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Benign</dt><dd>A tumor that stays in place and does not invade or spread.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Malignant</dt><dd>A tumor that invades neighbouring tissue and can spread. This is cancer.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Carcinoma in situ</dt><dd>Abnormal cells that have not yet crossed the basement membrane.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Metastasis</dt><dd>Spread of cancer cells through blood or lymph to start new tumors elsewhere.</dd></div>
  </dl>

  <h3 class="sub-h">Check yourself</h3>
  <div class="quiz">
    <details><summary>A friend has cancer. Can you catch it from them?</summary><p>No. Cancer is made of the patient's own cells and cannot take hold in another person's body through everyday contact. Some cancer-causing viruses can spread, but the cancer itself does not.</p></details>
    <details><summary>Why does cancer become more common with age?</summary><p>Cancer needs several hits to build up in one cell line. Each division adds a small chance of a new typo, so the longer you live, the more chances there are for the full set of hits to collect.</p></details>
    <details><summary>Oncogene versus tumor suppressor, in one line each?</summary><p>An oncogene is an accelerator stuck in the ON position. A tumor suppressor is a brake that has been cut. Most cancers have both.</p></details>
    <details><summary>A tumor has abnormal cells that have not crossed the basement membrane. What stage is it?</summary><p>Carcinoma in situ (stage 3 in Fig 6). It cannot spread yet, which is why removing it at this stage usually cures it.</p></details>
  </div>

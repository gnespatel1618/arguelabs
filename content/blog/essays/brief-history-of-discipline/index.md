In September 1830, as his wife later told it, Victor Hugo locked away his own clothes.

He owed his publisher a novel. He had already missed one deadline, and the publisher now wanted it by February 1831. According to his biographer Graham Robb, every week of further delay would cost him 1,000 francs.

So Hugo bought a new bottle of ink and a grey woollen body-stocking, a shapeless all-in-one garment. Then he locked the rest of his clothes in the wardrobe.

A man dressed in a woollen sack cannot go to a dinner party. He cannot stroll to a café. He can only sit and write. In Robb's words, he would "enter his novel like a prison." On 15 January 1831, he finished *The Hunchback of Notre-Dame*.

The story comes from his wife's account, and it may have grown in the telling. But legends survive when they carry a truth.

If the story is even half true, Hugo did not trust his own willpower. And that may be exactly why he finished.

You know Hugo's problem. Yours is worse. His temptations were across Paris. Yours fit in your pocket, and they buzz.

So was Hugo a disciplined man, or a weak man with a clever trick? The answer takes a short history.

Along the way, we will meet two Mumbai schoolboys who once put on 664 runs together. The one with the bigger score played his last Test at 23. The other was still playing for India 18 years later.

And we will meet 28 small children in Rochester who suggest that impatience may not be a weakness at all. It may be a reasonable response to being lied to. In much of adult life, I will argue, the liar is us.

## 1. The fuel tank nobody could find

You have said it, probably this week. Late at night, phone in hand: "I just don't have the willpower."

For almost twenty years, science agreed with you.

In 1998, psychologists led by Roy Baumeister sat 67 hungry students in front of chocolate-chip cookies. Some were allowed to eat them. Others had to eat radishes instead. Then everyone was given puzzles that, secretly, could not be solved.

The cookie eaters kept trying for about 19 minutes. The radish eaters gave up after about 8.

The explanation seemed obvious. Resisting the cookies had used something up. Willpower, the researchers proposed, is a fuel tank, and every act of self-control burns a little. They called it *ego depletion*.

It was a comfortable story. Nobody blames a car for stopping when the tank is empty.

Then science did what science is supposed to do. It checked.

In 2016, 23 labs ran one carefully standardised test of the idea on 2,141 people. The effect was close to zero. In 2021, 36 labs tried again with more than 3,500 people, led this time by researchers who had *believed* in ego depletion. Their preregistered analyses found no evidence for it.

Even the fuel was a mirage. One theory said self-control runs on blood sugar. But when people merely rinsed their mouths with sugar water and spat it out, their self-control improved, in one study as much as when they drank it. Nothing reached the tank. Something reached the motivation.

So why do we feel drained after a long day? The psychologist Michael Inzlicht has a better answer: "It's not that people cannot control themselves, it's that they don't feel like controlling themselves."

The tank was a wonderful story. It explained every failure and asked nothing of us. It just could not be found.

{{< chart src="f01-tank.svg" label="Figure 1" source="Baumeister et al. (1998); Hagger et al. (2016); Vohs et al. (2021)" >}}

Still, differences in self-control are real. In Dunedin, New Zealand, about 1,000 children were followed from birth to age 32, and childhood self-control predicted their health, their finances and even their criminal records. What failed was the explanation, not the differences.

So if you have spent years counting yourself among the small-tank people, drop the tank, not the question. Stop asking "How much willpower do I have?" Ask "Why don't I feel like it, and what can I change?"

Start with why it feels harder now than it did for your grandparents.

## 2. The most disciplined people fight less

For most of history, scarcity probably did much of the locking for us. There was no endless sugar, no endless news, no endless entertainment. The world itself was Hugo's wardrobe.

Now the wardrobe is wide open. So who still manages?

In 2012, researchers in Germany gave 205 adults devices that beeped seven times a day for a week. At about half the beeps, people wanted something. Nearly half of those desires clashed at least somewhat with another goal.

The people with the most self-control reported fewer of these clashes. They had fewer battles to begin with.

Think of the friend who seems effortlessly fit. You imagine her winning a war with the samosa every evening. She is probably not fighting at all. She just does not walk past the stall.

In six other studies of over 2,200 people, the more self-controlled held back their impulses *less* often. What linked their self-control to better grades, sleep and exercise was something boring: habits.

Are such people happy, or just tightly wound? A later study found self-control went hand in hand with happiness ("High self-control does make you happy," the authors concluded), partly because these people live with less inner conflict. We picture the disciplined at war with themselves. They look more like people who signed a ceasefire.

The disciplined do not win more fights with temptation. They pick fewer of them.

Part of the secret is attention. Starting around 1970, Walter Mischel and his colleagues ran a series of experiments asking young children to wait for a treat. With the treats in sight, they waited about 3 minutes. Hidden, nearly 9. The longest waiters covered their eyes, turned their backs, or sang. In later studies, children told to "put a frame around it in your head" waited almost 18 minutes.

They were not stronger. They were smarter about where to look.

{{< chart src="f02-attention.svg" label="Figure 2" source="Mischel and colleagues, via Stanford Bing Nursery School" >}}

In *Homo Deus*, Yuval Noah Harari wrote that "today having power means knowing what to ignore." For years he refused to own a smartphone. He did not win a daily duel with a phone. He simply declined to carry the opponent around.

Willpower is not useless. In one study that tracked 4,462 desires, plain resistance worked about as well as walking away. But the disciplined seem to need it less often.

There are two ways to fight less. You can change the world around the choice, or change the person making it. Hugo changed his room. One of the oldest versions of his trick is about 2,700 years old, and most retellings skip its best detail.

## 3. Who holds your rope?

Everyone remembers the rope. Almost nobody remembers the wax.

Odysseus had to sail past the Sirens, whose song lured sailors to their deaths. The sorceress Circe gave him a plan, and it was blunt: "should you beg your crew to free you, let them only bind you more tightly." He pressed softened beeswax into the ears of his crew and had them tie him to the mast.

The song came. He begged. In Homer's telling, the crew "rose and tightened my bonds and added more rope."

Look at the deck again. There were two kinds of discipline on that ship, and neither was willpower. The crew could not hear the song: Mischel's children covering their eyes. Odysseus heard every note and could not move: a decision made in advance, by a calmer man.

The only man who could hear the music was the only man who could not move.

Nobody on that ship was strong enough to resist the Sirens. The ship had been arranged so that nobody had to be.

Which one was the real Odysseus? The calm man who gave the order, or the desperate man who begged to be set free? Each of us is less a single person than a committee, and the members do not agree. Discipline is the art of letting the calm member vote first.

Two more details are easy to miss. The plan was not even his idea. And he did not tie the knots himself. You can tie your own rope, as Hugo did. But the hardest ropes to untie are held by someone else.

In February 1988, in a Harris Shield semi-final on Azad Maidan in Mumbai, two schoolboys put on those 664 runs unbroken, at the time a world record for any wicket in any class of cricket. Vinod Kambli made 349 not out. His friend Sachin Tendulkar made 326 not out.

Kambli's start in Test cricket was dazzling: 224 against England, then 227 against Zimbabwe in his very next Test, the first Indian to score double hundreds in consecutive Tests. He reached 1,000 Test runs faster than any Indian before him, and averaged 54.20.

He played his last Test in November 1995, at the age of 23.

Think about where you were at 23. Most of us were just getting started.

Tendulkar would play for India for another 18 years.

In 2014, Tendulkar was asked about the difference. "If we have to talk about differences, then I would say that his lifestyle was different." About himself: "In my case, my family was always there to keep an eye and keep me grounded at all times."

Careers end for many reasons: form, selectors, luck, and things outsiders never see. I won't pretend one sentence explains Kambli's. But notice what Tendulkar said in those lines. Not that he was more talented, or wanted it more. He talked about lifestyle, and about the family that kept him grounded. This is not a story about a good boy and a bad boy. It is about how much it helps to have someone holding the rope.

Talent opens the door. Something else decides how long you stay in the room.

And most of us, alone at 1 a.m. with a phone in hand, have nobody holding ours. Not because nobody would. Because we never asked.

## 4. Ropes made of money

Every month, roughly 10 crore SIP accounts in India tie their owners to a mast. In October 2026 alone, about ₹32,160 crore flowed through them.

Picture just one of them. Someone decided, once, on a quiet Sunday, that ₹2,000 would leave her account every month before she could argue with herself. That is the whole trick. When Richard Thaler (later a Nobel laureate) and Shlomo Benartzi let employees commit in advance to saving part of their *future* pay rises, 78% signed up, and the average saving rate of those who signed up rose from 3.5% to 13.6% over 40 months.

But an SIP is a softer rope than Odysseus had. His crew would have tightened it when he begged. Your app will untie it in a few taps. And an SIP does not guarantee a return. What it guarantees is that the decision is made by the calm you, not the frightened one.

Traders have a mast too: the stop-loss order placed in the system before 9:15, while your hands are steady. Not the one you will "definitely put" once the trade is live and the song has started.

Then, in February 2025, the Sirens sang.

You know what they sound like. Red screens. A WhatsApp forward saying the crash has only begun. A friend who sold everything and looks clever for a week.

The market fell about 5% that month. About 54.7 lakh SIPs ended, and only about 44.6 lakh began. For every 100 ropes tied, about 122 came loose. It was a record.

Be careful with that number. It includes SIPs that simply matured, which is no failure at all. Even last month, in October 2026, about 116 ended for every 100 that began. So the gap between the crash month and that one was about six or seven ropes in a hundred. February is a clue, not a verdict. But if some of those six or seven were cut in fear, they were cut in the one month the rope was built for.

{{< chart src="f03-ropes.svg" label="Figure 3" source="Thaler &amp; Benartzi (2004); AMFI data via Business Today and Outlook Money" >}}

Odysseus at least had the excuse of an enchanted song. Some of us may have had nothing grander than a WhatsApp forward.

The song does not need to be beautiful. It only needs to arrive when we are afraid.

Why would a sensible person cut the rope at exactly the wrong moment? A group of three-to-five-year-olds in Rochester has a clue.

## 5. Impatience is distrust in disguise

Every act of discipline is a bet on the future. Humans began placing that bet in earnest when they began to farm: food given up today for a harvest months away. Saving money, studying for an exam, skipping the second plate: each is the farmer's bet in new clothes.

The bet has two weak points. The first: the harvest goes to someone you barely know.

In a Stanford brain-scan study of just 18 people, thinking about one's future self looked more like thinking about someone else. So Hal Hershfield and his colleagues arranged an introduction. In virtual reality, people saw an aged version of their own face. Out of an imaginary $1,000, they then set aside $172 for retirement, against $80 for those shown their current face. A small study, with imaginary money. But meeting the stranger made them set more aside for that stranger.

Remember the committee on Odysseus's ship? Your future self is its most important member, and the only one who never gets a vote.

The second weak point is worse. Even if you care about the stranger, you have to believe the harvest will come.

You have heard of the marshmallow test, often treated as a test of character. In 2018, once researchers accounted for family background, its predictions about later life got much weaker. In 2013, a team at the University of Rochester found a better lesson in it.

Each child got an art project and a set of crayons. An adult promised to come back with better supplies. For half the children, the adult returned with them. For the other half, the adult came back empty-handed. Then came the familiar offer: one treat now, or wait and get more.

The children who had met a reliable adult waited about 12 minutes on average. The children who had been let down waited about 3. Nine of the fourteen in the reliable group held out for the full 15 minutes. In the other group, one did.

Only 28 children took part. But the gap was enormous: four times the waiting. The only thing the researchers changed was whether an adult kept a promise.

{{< chart src="f04-rational-snacking.svg" label="Figure 4" source="Kidd, Palmeri &amp; Aslin (2013), &quot;Rational snacking&quot;" >}}

They titled their paper *Rational snacking*. Those children were being reasonable. Why sacrifice today for a promise from a world that has already lied to you?

Now look at your own life through their eyes. The gym from Monday. The course after Diwali. No phone after 11. How many times did that adult fail to show up with the crayons?

We have been unreliable adults to ourselves.

My guess is that every broken promise to yourself teaches the child inside you the same small lesson: the future does not pay, so eat the marshmallow now.

That study did not test adults, or promises we make to ourselves. The rest is my reading, not a result. But think of it the way a lender would. Every promise you make to yourself is a small loan from your future self. Repay it, and the next one is believed. Default often enough, and your future self stops lending. That is not weakness. That is credit risk.

The hopeful part is in the experiment too. A few minutes with one adult, reliable or not, made a fourfold difference in how long a child would wait. That is their finding. Mine is a hope: if trust can fall that fast, kept promises may rebuild it just as fast, one small repayment at a time.

Keep enough of those promises and something larger changes. Not what you do. Who you think you are.

## 6. Can't is a rule. Don't is an identity.

After the 2012 IPL, a young Indian batsman came back home and, in his words, "saw myself, I was disgusted. I just wanted to change everything about myself."

Virat Kohli had been eating, as he put it, "like a mad person." A pack of 40 toffees could vanish in four or five days. He gave up desserts, soft drinks, gluten and wheat. For many Indians, giving up wheat means giving up roti. His explanation was four words long: "It's for my discipline." By 2016, he put it differently to ESPNcricinfo: "I made a lifestyle choice." And: "Now it has become second nature to me."

The toffees had not become less sweet. Kohli had become someone who did not eat them.

Even a single word can nudge that shift. In one small study, 64% of people taught to refuse temptation with "I don't" later picked a granola bar over chocolate, against 39% of those taught to say "I can't". *Can't* is a rule someone imposed on you. *Don't* is a statement about who you are.

A sturdier finding goes further. In a set of experiments, "please don't be a *cheater*" reduced cheating more than "please don't *cheat*." When researchers repeated the first experiment in 2020, it held up.

Nobody wants to become a noun they despise.

Long before any of these studies, a young Mohandas Gandhi wanted to go to England. His mother let him go only after he vowed "not to touch wine, woman, and meat." He built his life on vows, and had no patience for half-promises: "A man who says that he will do something 'as far as possible', betrays either his pride or his weakness."

Every "I'll try to go to the gym this week" is an "as far as possible." It is a loan with no repayment date, and most of us take one out every January.

"I realized," Gandhi wrote of a later vow, "that a vow, far from closing the door to real freedom, opened it." Settle a choice once, and you stop paying for it every morning.

But a vow has a weakness of its own. It shows up on the day you break it.

## 7. The day after you fall

A bright line is strong because it is all-or-nothing. That is also how it breaks.

In 1975, the psychologists Peter Herman and Deborah Mack found out what happens to a dieter on the day the line is crossed. Before a fake ice-cream taste test, they gave some people one or two milkshakes. Non-dieters sensibly ate less ice cream afterwards. Dieters ate *more*. The diet was already broken, so why hold back now? Janet Polivy later nicknamed it the *what-the-hell effect*. In Hindi: *ab toh diet toot hi gayi, ek aur kha lo*.

Every trader knows it too. One bad loss before lunch and the day is already "ruined," so you double the lot size to win it back by 3:30. It is the milkshake, followed by the whole tub.

Addiction researcher Alan Marlatt saw the darker version: a person slips once, blames a permanent flaw (*I have no willpower*), and that story makes it far more likely one lapse becomes a full relapse.

The slip itself is rarely the disaster. The story we tell about the slip is.

Put it in a lender's terms. A missed EMI is a missed EMI. Declaring bankruptcy over it is the real ruin.

So decide in advance what a crossed line means: a slip, not a new identity. "I am weak" is an identity too, the dark twin of "I don't."

Jerry Seinfeld is often credited with the advice "don't break the chain." In 2014 he called it "the dumbest non-idea that was not mine, but somehow I'm getting credit for it."

The science is kinder than the myth. At University College London, missing a single day barely dented how automatic a new habit became. The famous "66 days" is only a middle value, from the 39 of 96 people whose data fit the pattern. The range ran from 18 days to an estimated 254. If you are on day 40 and it still feels hard, you are not broken. You are normal.

Chains break. Habits can survive the break.

You might expect the cure to be harshness. The research, modest as it is, points the other way. At Berkeley, students who failed a hard vocabulary test and were nudged towards self-compassion went on to study about 50% longer than students given nothing (roughly five minutes versus three and a half). They even edged out students given a pep talk about how capable they were. Kindness did at least as well as flattery.

If you are reading this the day after you fell, with the streak broken or the SIP paused in a bad month, this is for you. Yesterday is one data point, not a verdict. Be kind, then be specific: what is the smallest version of the habit you can do today? Do that. That is the whole comeback.

## Back to the man in the woollen sack

Hugo wrote almost 200,000 words in about four and a half months, in his woollen sack of a garment, or so his wife told it.

So, was he a disciplined man, or a weak man with a clever trick?

He was a weak man with a clever trick. So was Odysseus. That is not the opposite of discipline. That is what discipline is.

Hugo, Odysseus, the children who covered their eyes: none of them relied on being strong in the moment. Each arranged things so the moment mattered less.

The world no longer does the locking for us. Ours may be the first generation that has to build its own wardrobe.

Every promise you keep to yourself is a small repayment to the child inside you. Keep enough of them, and you become the adult who came back with the crayons.

Willpower is the hope that you will be strong. Discipline is the admission that you won't, and the plan you make anyway.

Your wardrobe is in your pocket. Lock it.

## Save this: seven knots

1. **The wardrobe.** Put the temptation out of reach. The phone in another room beats the phone you are "not going to check."
2. **The mast.** Decide once, on a calm day. Automate the SIP on salary day. Place the stop-loss before 9:15, not in your head. (Not investment advice.)
3. **The crew.** Give one person your rule (your daily loss limit, your SIP date) and permission to ask about it. (My takeaway from Odysseus and Tendulkar, not a lab result.)
4. **The audiobook.** Chain the hard to the pleasant: a story you only hear at the gym. In one study, gym visits rose 51% at first, then faded. Yet 61% later chose to pay for gym-only access. People wanted the rope.
5. **The stranger.** Write three lines to yourself at 60. (My extrapolation from Hershfield, not a tested trick.)
6. **The vow.** Say "I don't," not "I can't." Nobody wants to become a quitter.
7. **The next morning.** A bad day is a missed EMI, not bankruptcy. Forgive it in one sentence, then do the smallest version of the habit.

**Start tonight.** Put your phone in another room. It is a promise small enough to keep, and keeping it is your first repayment. Then write: *If it is 7 a.m., I will \_\_\_.* Across 94 tests, if-then plans like this had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals. And you need not wait for January: gym visits jump 33% at the start of an ordinary week.

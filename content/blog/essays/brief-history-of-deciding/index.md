Shortly after midnight on 26 September 1983, a siren went off in a bunker outside Moscow.

The screen in front of Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov turned bright red. "It was piercing," he later said, "loud enough to raise a dead man from his grave." The Soviet Union's new early-warning satellites were reporting that the United States had launched a nuclear missile. Then another. Then five.

The timing could not have been worse. Less than four weeks earlier, the Soviets had shot down a South Korean airliner, killing 269 people. Relations with America were as cold as they had been in decades. Petrov's job was to pass the warning up the chain of command, where men with their fingers near the button would have only minutes to decide whether to fire back.

The computer was certain. Petrov was not.

He reported a false alarm. Then he waited, knowing that if he was wrong, American warheads would land before anyone could correct his mistake.

Nothing landed. The satellite had mistaken sunlight glinting off high clouds for the fire of rocket engines.

What saved the world that night — instinct or logic? A gut feeling, or cold reasoning? Keep that question in mind. Its answer reveals the most valuable skill a human being can still have in the age of artificial intelligence.

## Who gets to decide?

For most of history, humans did not trust themselves to make big decisions. Ancient Greeks climbed the mountain to Delphi and asked the oracle. Roman generals watched the flight of birds before a battle. Kings across Asia did not crown an heir or sign a treaty without consulting the astrologers. The decision was too heavy for a mere human, so it was handed to the gods.

Then, over a few centuries, something radical happened. Authority moved from heaven to the human heart. The voter knows best. The customer is always right. Follow your passion. For the first time, ordinary people were told that their own judgment was the final authority on their lives.

In *Homo Deus*, Yuval Noah Harari argues that authority is now moving again — this time from humans to algorithms. We already let an app choose our route home, our next film and the news we read. Now machines draft our contracts, write our code, summarise our earnings calls and scan five thousand stocks before we finish our morning tea.

The work of the mind — the work we thought made us special — is being taken over, one task at a time.

So what is left?

More than you think. A machine can tell you what is *likely* to happen. It cannot tell you what you *want*, what you can afford to lose, or when the machine itself is wrong. Those are not calculations. They are decisions. And as the thinking becomes cheap, the deciding becomes the whole game.

The trouble is that we are much worse at it than we believe.

## 1. A decision is not its outcome

Johannesburg, 2007, the first T20 World Cup final. Pakistan need 13 runs from the last over, with one wicket left. M. S. Dhoni throws the ball to Joginder Sharma, a little-known medium-pacer, instead of Harbhajan Singh. Misbah-ul-Haq smashes a full toss for six. Then he tries a scoop. The ball loops up to short fine leg, and Sreesanth catches it. India are champions. Dhoni is a genius.

Now imagine that scoop had cleared Sreesanth's head by two metres. Same captain, same bowler, same reasoning. Dhoni would have been a fool for a decade.

Eight years later, on the other side of the world, the coin landed the other way. In the 2015 Super Bowl — American football's biggest game — the Seattle Seahawks were one yard from victory with 26 seconds left. Coach Pete Carroll chose a pass instead of a run. It was intercepted. Seattle lost, and the press called it the worst call in Super Bowl history.

But no quarterback in the league had thrown an interception from the one-yard line all season. The pass also used up a down without wasting the clock. Statisticians later calculated that the call had slightly *increased* Seattle's chances of winning. Carroll put it perfectly: "It was the worst result of a call ever."

The former professional poker player Annie Duke has a name for our habit of judging decisions by how they turned out. She calls it *resulting*.

The stock market is the world's largest resulting machine. Buy a penny stock on a WhatsApp tip and double your money, and you feel like a genius. Do months of research, buy a sound company, lose money in a crash, and you feel like an idiot. The market rewards bad decisions often enough to teach millions of people exactly the wrong lessons.

In a world of uncertainty, a good outcome is not proof of a good decision. It is only a good outcome.

## 2. The enemy sits inside your skull

Our brains were shaped on the savannah, where the cost of a mistake was usually death. A rustle in the grass might be the wind or a lion. The ancestor who assumed *lion* and ran survived. The one who calmly weighed the probabilities was eaten. We are descended from the nervous ones.

So we inherited a mind that hates losing far more than it loves winning. In 1992, the psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman measured it: on average, people needed a possible gain of about *twice* the possible loss before they would accept a fifty-fifty bet. A loss of ₹1,000 hurts roughly as much as a gain of ₹2,000 pleases.

Watch what this does to an investor. In 1998, the economist Terrance Odean studied 10,000 accounts at a large American brokerage. Investors sold their winning stocks about one and a half times as readily as their losing ones. They grabbed small gains quickly and clung to losses, hoping to "get back to break-even." And the winners they sold went on to beat the losers they kept by about 3.4 percentage points over the following year.

It gets worse. Even trained experts disagree with themselves far more than they realise. When a large insurance company had its underwriters price identical cases, the median gap between two quotes was 55%. Senior executives, asked how much experts would disagree, had guessed about 10%. Same facts, same firm, wildly different decisions. Kahneman called this *noise*.

{{< chart src="f02-bias-noise.svg" label="Figure 2" source="Tversky &amp; Kahneman (1992); the insurance underwriter study via strategy+business" >}}

Now look at India's futures and options market. In September 2024, SEBI studied over 1 crore individual F&O traders across three years, FY22 to FY24. About 93% lost money. Their combined losses exceeded ₹1.8 lakh crore — roughly ₹2 lakh per trader. And more than 75% of the loss-makers kept trading anyway.

So who made money? In FY24 alone, proprietary trading firms booked gross profits of about ₹33,000 crore and foreign portfolio investors about ₹28,000 crore. Here is the detail that should keep every retail trader awake: 97% of those FPI profits and 96% of those proprietary profits came from *algorithmic trading*.

{{< chart src="f03-fno.svg" label="Figure 3" source="SEBI study of individual F&amp;O traders, September 2024" >}}

The great contest between humans and machines is not coming to Dalal Street. It has already been played. The machines did not win because they are wiser than us. They won because they feel nothing. No fear after a loss. No greed after a win. No revenge trade at 3:15 p.m. They simply do not have our savannah brain.

## 3. Brilliant models, terrible decisions

If feelings are the problem, surely the answer is to remove them: hire the smartest people, build the best models, and let mathematics decide.

In 1994, a legendary bond trader named John Meriwether founded a hedge fund called Long-Term Capital Management. On its board sat Myron Scholes and Robert Merton, who in 1997 would win the Nobel Prize in Economics for their work on pricing options. Their models said the fund's trades carried very little risk. So they borrowed — more than 25 dollars for every dollar of their own.

In 1998, Russia defaulted on its debt, and the world did something the models said it almost never does. In under four months, LTCM lost $4.6 billion. In September, the New York Federal Reserve gathered a group of banks to put up $3.6 billion in a rescue, fearing the fund's collapse would drag down the financial system.

The models were not stupid. They were right most of the time. The fatal error was not in the mathematics but in the decision built on top of it: to treat *unlikely* as *impossible*, and to bet the firm on that confidence.

Machines have their own version of this. On the morning of 1 August 2012, Knight Capital, then one of America's biggest market makers, deployed new trading software. An old piece of code on one server woke up by mistake and began buying and selling millions of shares. There was no kill switch. In about 45 minutes, Knight lost $440 million — roughly $10 million a minute. Within hours, the firm was fighting for its survival.

Petrov's satellites, LTCM's equations, Knight's algorithm — all three were sophisticated. All three were wrong. And in each case, the danger grew with the trust placed in them.

This is the second-order trap of intelligence. The smarter a system looks, the bigger the bet we are willing to place on it. A model rarely ruins anyone on its own. It needs a human who believes it too much.

## 4. Cheap prediction, priceless judgment

So humans are biased and machines can be blind. Where does that leave us in an age when AI is taking over the work of the mind?

Three economists from the University of Toronto — Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb — offered the clearest answer in their 2018 book *Prediction Machines*. Strip away the hype, they argued, and AI does one thing: prediction. Which customer will leave. Which X-ray shows a tumour. Which stock is likely to beat its earnings estimate. And AI is making prediction cheap.

When something becomes cheap, whatever goes with it becomes valuable. The partner of prediction is *judgment*: deciding what each possible outcome is worth to you, and which goal you are actually pursuing. "The value of human judgment will increase," they wrote, "because we'll need more of it."

An AI can read every annual report on the NSE before lunch. It can estimate the odds that a stock doubles. It cannot tell you whether you need the money in two years or twenty, whether you can sleep through a 40% fall, or whether the opportunity is worth the risk *for you*. That is not prediction. That is judgment, and it remains your job.

But there is a darker possibility.

On 1 June 2009, Air France Flight 447 was crossing the Atlantic at night when ice blocked its speed sensors and the autopilot switched off. The plane was still flyable. But the pilots, used to letting the computer fly, misread the situation and pulled the nose up into a stall. All 228 people on board died. French investigators later pointed to an absence of training in manual flying at high altitude. A few years later, a report for the US aviation regulator warned that pilots had allowed basic manual skills to erode as automation took over.

The same pattern is now appearing in offices. In a 2025 study of 319 knowledge workers by Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon, the more people trusted generative AI, the less critical thinking they reported doing. Another small 2025 experiment at MIT found that people who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed the weakest brain connectivity of any group — and struggled to quote the essays they had just "written."

The real danger of AI is not that it will make our decisions badly. It is that we will forget how to make them — and lose the ability to notice when the machine is wrong. Petrov could overrule his satellites only because he understood them well enough to doubt them.

## 5. Seven habits of a good decision-maker

You cannot rewire a savannah brain. But you can build a few habits around it, the way a sailor cannot change the wind but can set the sails. Here are seven that the best investors, scientists and leaders keep coming back to.

1. **Judge the process, not the result.** Before any important decision — a trade, a job, an investment — write down why you are making it and what you expect. Later, compare. A decision journal is the only cure for resulting, because memory rewrites itself to make you look smart.
2. **Ask: is this a one-way door or a two-way door?** Jeff Bezos split decisions into two types. Some are "consequential and irreversible" — one-way doors — and deserve slow, careful thought. Most are two-way doors: "you can reopen the door and go back through." Move fast on those. Most people do the opposite: they agonise over small reversible choices and rush the irreversible ones.
3. **Invert.** Charlie Munger loved a line from the mathematician Carl Jacobi: "Invert, always invert." Instead of asking *how do I succeed?*, ask *what would guarantee failure?* — then avoid it. As Munger joked: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there."
4. **Run a premortem.** Before you commit, imagine it is a year later and the decision has failed badly. Now explain why. The psychologist Gary Klein popularised this trick, citing research showing that this kind of "prospective hindsight" improves people's ability to identify reasons for future outcomes by 30%.
5. **Start with the base rate.** Before asking *will I make money?*, ask *how many people like me do?* For individual F&O traders in India from FY22 to FY24, the answer was about 7 in 100. Your story about why you are different should be very good.
6. **Size the bet to how wrong you could be.** LTCM's models were mostly right. Their leverage assumed they could never be badly wrong. Never let a single decision — or a single model — be able to wipe you out.
7. **Let the machine predict. You decide.** Use AI for what it is brilliant at: gathering facts, running numbers, steelmanning the opposite view. Then close the laptop and write the final call in your own words, with your own reasons. If you cannot explain the decision without the machine, it is not yet your decision.

## Back to the bunker

So what saved the world in September 1983 — instinct or logic?

Listen to how Petrov explained it. It seemed odd that an American first strike would begin with just five missiles. He did not fully trust the new detection system, which had only recently been installed. And ground radars, minutes later, still showed nothing. That was logic: base rates, knowledge of the machine's limits, and a search for confirming evidence.

But he also said this: "I had a funny feeling in my gut. I didn't want to make a mistake. I made a decision and that was it."

The answer is both. Good judgment is not the victory of reason over feeling, or of feeling over reason. It is a mind that knows enough to doubt the machine, and is honest enough to doubt itself. Petrov also understood the shape of the bet. Calling a real attack a false alarm would cost a few minutes of warning. Calling a false alarm an attack could start a nuclear war that never needed to happen.

And here is the final irony. Petrov was not rewarded for his decision. He was promised a reward that never came, then reprimanded over paperwork and reassigned. Institutions are very good at measuring whether you followed the procedure. They are very bad at measuring whether you made the right call.

The coming decades will give us more answers than any generation in history. Machines will read every document, price every risk and forecast every outcome. The scarce thing will be the person willing to look at the screen and say: I have seen the numbers. I have weighed the odds. Now I decide.

Machines will increasingly tell us what is likely. Only we can decide what is worth it.

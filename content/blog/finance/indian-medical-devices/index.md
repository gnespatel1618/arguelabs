## The 60-second view

> India's medical-device story is a real import-substitution story, but it sits mostly in plastic and steel disposables and mid-technology assembly, the margin pools are at the maker and the hospital, and the only scaled listed pure-play, Poly Medicure, is priced for its best case.

{{< chart src="m01-at-a-glance.svg" label="At a glance" source="IBEF, IMARC, Trade.gov, Business Today; Screener.in · 23 Oct 2026; growth need from my reverse DCF" >}}

It is written as an investment-committee memo: what must be true, what is priced in, and what would prove it wrong.

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| Where do the margins lie? | Makers whose products need approvals and clinical evidence earn 24% to 28% EBITDA (Poly); importers and distributors earn about 4% to 10%; hospital billing carries the widest mark-ups, up to about 29x purchase price on consumables |
| What drives the next phase? | Import substitution (domestic share of local demand from about 10% to about 30% in five years), hospital capacity (about 38,000 private beds by 2030), Tier II-III demand and exports. Growth reaches profit only where the product moves up: Poly's FY26 sales grew 12% while net profit fell 5% |
| How do Indian and global players compare? | Indian makers win on cost and bought capability in mid-complexity categories; R&D is 1.6% of sales at Poly against a 7.3% global median, and the largest listed maker is about 1/165th of Medtronic |
| What can India make? | Moulded, extruded and assembled disposables competitively; stents, dialysers and imaging only with imported critical inputs; local content is about 30% |
| Which listed names track it? | Poly Medicure is the only scaled, liquid pure-play; the rest are small caps, SME listings or mixed models, with trailing P/Es from 22x to 135x |
| What can break it? | Price first (trade-margin caps, Chinese dumping, tariffs, de-rating), then the thin component base |
| What is priced in? | At Rs 1,444 Poly needs about 35% revenue growth to FY30 or a 9.1% discount rate on my base case; a 12% annual return needs about 16% EPS growth and a 40x exit P/E; the sum of the parts gives Rs 1,351 (range Rs 1,084 to Rs 1,629) |

What India can and cannot make explains most of the rest. It makes IV sets, cannulas, syringes, needles, blood bags, gloves and labware competitively, because the value sits in tooling, automated lines and sterile supply chains rather than in the polymer. It assembles stents, dialysers, ultrasound and X-ray systems, but imports what sits underneath: medical-grade resins, specialty alloys, membranes, X-ray tubes and detectors, sensors, semiconductors and magnets. Imports were about $8.8 billion against $4 billion of exports in FY25. The second-order consequence is that the listed winners today are the integrated assemblers, not the suppliers beneath them, and that in my reading a margin cap on the channel would reach makers last.

{{< chart src="n01-trade-gap.svg" label="Figure 1" source="Business Today (Aug 2026), GrowthJockey, IBEF · FY25" >}}

The committee question is therefore two questions. Is the sector worth owning? The runway for import substitution says yes, and the policy and demand drivers back it. Is the stock worth its price? For Poly the price leaves no cushion: it asks for roughly twice the growth I would underwrite, or a margin double today's, and it gives little visible discount to the policy, tariff and dumping risks listed above. The kill criteria for the sector thesis and for the Poly case are in the final section.

## Products and how they are made

Indian makers are strongest where a product is moulded, extruded or assembled from imported plastic and steel inputs, and weakest where it needs a membrane, a precision sensor, a magnet or a specialty chemical.

Two things to take from this table. First, the moat in disposables is not the polymer: it is the mould tool room, the automated line and the sterile, regulated supply chain, which is why a 340-patent maker earns 24% to 28% EBITDA while commodity syringe lines compete on price. Second, the same table shows where margins are capped: whoever supplies the resin, the membrane or the tube sets the cost floor, and in India that supplier is usually foreign.

## India's manufacturing capability map

India is a competitive maker of plastic and steel disposables, an assembler of most mid-technology devices, and a net importer of the components, membranes, sensors and magnets that sit underneath them: imports were about $8.8 billion against $4 billion of exports in FY25, and local content is only about 30%.

{{< chart src="n02-capability-tiers.svg" label="Figure 2" source="Business Today, India Briefing, GrowthJockey; tier placement is my synthesis" >}}

| Tier | What India does | Examples | Why the line sits there |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Makes competitively, from imported polymer and steel | Moulding, extrusion, automated assembly, EO sterilisation | IV sets, cannulas, syringes, needles, blood bags, gloves, labware, basic dental products | Low capital per unit, volume learning curve, mould and tool-room skill; Chinese and ASEAN prices set the ceiling |
| 2. Makes the device, imports the critical input | Final assembly, coating, calibration, regulatory file | Stents (SMT imported 77% of FY26 purchases), dialysers (membrane), ultrasound and X-ray systems (tubes, detectors, probes), monitors (sensors and chips), analysers (optics, reagents) | The input needs microfabrication, polymer chemistry or biochemistry that few Indian suppliers have; assembly captures only 30-40% of product value |
| 3. Mostly imports | Distribution and service | Advanced MRI and CT, robotic surgery, advanced endoscopes, pacemakers, continuous glucose monitors, high-end diagnostic analysers | R&D of 7 to 10 years, aerospace-grade precision, clinical-evidence base, hospital brand preference; the government has listed MRI, CT and X-ray components, advanced ultrasound, pacemakers, CGM, ICU beds and high-end analysers for localisation review |
| Components under all tiers | Little | Medical-grade resins, specialty alloys, precision motors, sensors, semiconductors, validated reagents | Supplier depth is shallow; duties on sub-assemblies can exceed duties on finished goods, so importing a whole system is sometimes cheaper than sourcing modules locally |

Sources: [Business Today, Aug 2026](https://www.businesstoday.in/magazine/deep-dive/story/why-indias-medical-devices-manufacturing-has-seen-limited-success-549744-2026-08-18), [India Briefing](https://www.india-briefing.com/news/india-medtech-localization-manufacturing-investment-opportunities-45813.html/), [GrowthJockey: supply-chain tiers](https://www.growthjockey.com/blogs/mapping-the-medtech-supply-chain), [GrowthJockey: import addiction](https://www.growthjockey.com/blogs/import-addiction-india-medtech), [SMT summary](https://www.incredmoney.com/blog/sahajanand-medical-technologies-ipo/). Tier placement of individual products is my synthesis of these sources; none gives a product-by-product import share.

What shapes the map:

- **Concentrated import bill:** imports span about 6,000 devices across 160 HS codes, and 40 codes account for roughly 85% of the bill. Localising a few dozen products would move the total.
- **Cost structure works against assemblers:** most devices carry 5% GST while many inputs and job work carry 18%; ASEAN goods enter at zero tariff and Chinese product is alleged to be routed through Malaysia and Vietnam; public tenders often pick the lowest bid.
- **Policy is real but small against the gap:** the PLI scheme covers 55 high-end devices with a 5% incentive on incremental sales for a five-year performance period (FY23 to FY27); 28 of 77 applications were approved and 22 projects have started, with Rs 12,344 cr of sales and Rs 5,869 cr of exports reported. Medical device parks add shared testing, calibration and EO sterilisation; reported counts differ (four operating parks in one source, nine named sites in another, including AMTZ at Visakhapatnam, Telangana, Oragadam in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and the UP park near Noida).
- **Government targets:** import dependence below 40% and output of $30 billion by 2030 (cited by GrowthJockey); these are aims, not forecasts.
- **The second-order point:** a component maker needs volume to justify the capital, and that volume exists only if many assemblers buy locally; the reverse is also true. That is why clusters and procurement preference matter more than a single plant, and why the listed winners are today the integrated assemblers rather than the suppliers beneath them.

## The value chain and where the margins lie

Margin follows scarcity: it sits with makers whose products need approvals and clinical evidence and with hospital billing, while the importer or distributor in between earns a thin 4% to 10%.

{{< chart src="a01-value-chain.svg" label="Figure 3" source="My synthesis of the sourced figures in this section · 5 stages" >}}

Start at the left. Components are the one stage where this note has no sourced margin, but it does have the dependence: Poly Medicure imported 60% to 70% of its raw materials in FY22-23 and SMT's imports were 77% of its FY26 purchases, so part of the manufacturing margin is spent abroad before the product exists. At the manufacturer, margin tracks how hard the product is to copy. A maker of IV sets or syringes sells into a crowded field with little clinical lock-in; a maker of stents, cardiology devices or dialysers needs approvals and clinical evidence, which is what supports Poly's 24% to 28% EBITDA and Medtronic's 24.4% operating margin. Geography matters too: a 2023 HDFC Securities note put Poly's domestic EBITDA margin at 18% to 20% against about 30% on exports. That is one older source I could not refresh, so read it as direction, not level.

{{< chart src="a02-margin-ladder.svg" label="Figure 4" source="Screener.in, company releases, Mercer Capital, CARE Ratings · 10 companies, latest period each" >}}

Two readings survive the definitional mismatch. First, the distribution-type names with data earn single digits to about 10% (Meril India's distribution arm 10.4%, Vasa Denticity 6.1%), and that thin margin has to fund inventory and receivables. Second, SMT at 14.1% sits far below the 27.1% global median even though it makes stents, so in my reading it is scale and product mix, not an Indian cost advantage, that sets a maker's margin.

The largest spread has no audited margin attached to it. A Maharashtra price-monitoring survey of inpatient consumables found a standard IV infusion set bought at Rs 11.05 and billed at an MRP of Rs 325 (a 2,841% mark-up), a 10 ml syringe at Rs 6.75 against Rs 57.20 (747%) and a disposable mask at 153%. The industry body AiMeD cites cardiac catheters landed at Rs 1,500 to 2,000 against MRPs of Rs 7,000 to 11,000. That spread is why policy attention is moving to the channel: on 18 September the health minister asked the Department of Pharmaceuticals to evaluate trade-margin rationalisation, and a parliamentary committee suggested capping the landing-price-to-MRP gap at 20% (the article does not print the year, so I infer 2026). Precedents exist: stent and knee-implant price caps in 2017 and a 70% trade-margin cap on five device types in July 2021.

{{< chart src="n03-markups.svg" label="Figure 5" source="Maharashtra price-monitoring survey via Medical Buyer; AiMeD via Business Today (Apr 2026)" >}}

My inference, not a policy fact: if a cap is set at the first point of sale, the squeeze lands on importers, distributors and hospital billing, and a manufacturer selling at ex-factory prices is hit less directly. The second-order effect is the one to watch. A lower MRP ceiling removes the room that lets a hospital absorb a tender discount, so price pressure can travel back to the maker at the next tender, and makers of commodity consumables would feel it before makers of differentiated implants.

## What will drive the next phase of growth

The next phase is driven more by import substitution, product-mix upgrades and exports than by the market simply getting bigger, which matters because published market-size forecasts imply growth anywhere from about 6% to about 27% a year.

Three things follow. First, growth converts to profit only where the product has moved up the value ladder: Poly's FY26 sales grew 12% but net profit fell 5%, and four of the original five proxies had lower profit on higher sales. Second, the fastest-growing lines (cardiology, structural heart, trauma) are the ones with the longest approval and clinical lead times, so growth arrives in steps tied to certifications rather than smoothly. Third, a market growing 15% to 20% with import substitution adds roughly 1.5 to 2 times that rate to a domestic maker's addressable share, but only until local share reaches the ceiling set by the components India cannot yet make.

{{< chart src="n06-india-vs-global.svg" label="Figure 8" source="Screener.in, Poly Medicure Q1 FY27 slides, Tijori; Mercer Capital (to 31 Mar 2026)" >}}

The Indian edge is real but narrow. Single-use consumables (infusion sets, cannulas, syringes, blood bags) are high-volume, process-driven products where Indian cost levels and export approvals matter more than invention, and Indian Class-C devices are priced 10% to 40% below imports. That is where Poly built its position. The edge fades as the product gets harder: the advanced devices India makes locally are about a fifth of its Class-C demand, imaging is mostly imported, and a 1.6% R&D budget cannot fund a frontier pipeline. Buying capability is the shortcut Poly has taken, with PendraCare (Netherlands, interventional cardiology catheters, EUR 18.8 million for 90%) and Citieffe (Italy, trauma implants, about EUR 31 million) in 2025.

Second-order read: the global leaders are not the only competitors. Chinese makers set the price in commodity lines (Poly's renal sales fell 3.8% on Chinese pricing), and unlisted Indian champions such as Meril compete in the same mid-complexity categories where SMT and Poly are trying to move up. So an Indian listed maker's real contest is a price war at the bottom and a certification race at the top, and the valuation premium of about two times the global multiple pays for winning the second while surviving the first.

How to use the list:

## Key risks and threats to the story

The most probable source of near-term losses is not demand but price: a trade-margin cap, Chinese dumping, a tariff, or a de-rating of premium multiples; the structural risk is that India stays an assembler of imported components.

## Valuations: what's priced in, and what isn't

At Rs 1,444 Poly Medicure's price already pays for Vision 2030 and a premium multiple that lasts, while it gives little visible credit for the channel-margin, tariff and dumping risks; the smaller proxies need margins well above today's to justify their prices.

A reverse DCF solves for what each price needs (growth, margin or discount rate) without producing a fair value. A return-hurdle grid asks what earnings growth and exit multiple a target return needs. Peer multiples show where each stock sits against the group and the global median. A sum of the parts for Poly Medicure prices its segments separately. Every forward number is my assumption and is marked (E).

### What the price of each proxy needs

{{< chart src="a03-implied-margin.svg" label="Figure 12" source="Screener.in EV and operating profit (23 Oct 2026); reverse-DCF solve is mine · 8 listed proxies" >}}

Each row holds the stylised reverse DCF to the same shape: revenue compounds at a constant 15% or 20% for ten years, the margin stays constant, tax is 25%, cost of capital runs from 12.5% (Poly) to 14.5% (smallest names) by size, terminal growth is 5.5%, and reinvestment equals D&A plus half of incremental revenue plus working capital of 22% of incremental revenue. The bar is the margin that makes the discounted cash flows equal today's enterprise value. It tests plausibility; it does not forecast.

Poly needs 40% to 50% against 23% earned, and the global device median is 27.1%, so the price needs a margin that few device makers anywhere earn. Tarsons and Molbio need even more, and Tarsons' depreciation of 24% of sales is a large part of why. Only Q-Line Biotech sits within a few points of its required margin, and it is a small SME-listed name with thin data. The stocks that look cheapest on EV/Sales (Vasa, Laxmi, SMT) are not cheap on this test, because their current margins are far below what the price needs.

### What is priced in and what is not

| Theme | What the price assumes | What would change it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth | Revenue compounds well above Vision 2030's 19% a year (35% to FY30 on the reverse DCF) | Q2 to Q4 FY27 must average about Rs 592 cr a quarter against Rs 525 cr in Q1 just to reach the Rs 2,300 cr floor |
| Margin | EBITDA margin holds at 23% to 25% while Citieffe and PendraCare are integrated | Q1 FY27 net profit fell 8.6% on 30% sales growth; watch the margin against 24% |
| Multiple | About 27x FY27E EBITDA (32x trailing on operating profit) against 13.7x globally and 14x to 24x for Indian peers | A growth slip tests the multiple first: five turns of exit P/E is worth about Rs 185 a share |
| Policy and trade | Little visible discount, in my read, for trade-margin caps, the undecided US Section 232 probe or the PLI scheme ending in FY27 | The DoP outcome on where a cap applies; a Section 232 determination |
| Renal and dumping | Management's 15% to 18% renal growth for FY27 | Renal fell 3.8% in Q1 on Chinese pricing; an anti-dumping duty decision is expected around year-end |
| Pipeline | Small: the sum of the parts gives cardiology about Rs 57 a share | PACIFER DES registry (840 of 2,000 enrolled) and the orthopaedic ramp-up |

For the price to earn 12% a year, several things must hold together: revenue near my base-case Rs 3,528 cr by FY30, EBITDA margin at 24% or better, an exit multiple still near 40x P/E, channel rules that stop short of ex-factory prices, and renal pricing that stabilises. Miss any one and nothing in the price absorbs it. At my base-case EPS growth and a 30x exit, the price that earns 12% is Rs 1,112, about 23% below today. This is analysis of what the price implies, not a recommendation to buy or sell; I am not a licensed adviser.

## What to watch

The thesis breaks on price rules and on Poly's execution before it breaks on demand, so the watch-list is built around the levels at which I would change my view. The thresholds are my judgement, not published targets.

| Signal | Level that would change my view | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where a trade-margin cap applies | A cap set at ex-factory prices rather than at the first point of sale | It would move the squeeze from the channel to the makers (sector thesis) |
| Poly revenue, FY27 | Below Rs 2,300 cr (Q2 to Q4 must average about Rs 592 cr a quarter against Rs 525 cr in Q1) | The price needs growth well above Vision 2030's 19% a year |
| Poly EBITDA margin | Below 22% for two consecutive quarters (guidance 23% to 25%; Q1 FY27 consolidated 24.1%) | Reverse DCF needs a far higher margin, so any slip widens the gap |
| Renal growth | Still negative after an anti-dumping duty decision (due around year-end) | Shows whether Chinese pricing is cyclical or structural |
| Exit multiple | Poly trades below about 30x trailing P/E on a flat EPS path | At 30x the 12%-return entry price is Rs 1,112, about 23% below today |
| Domestic share of local demand | Stalls near 30% for two years | The import-substitution runway is the sector case |
| Local content | Flat near 30% while imports keep rising | Assembly without component depth caps margins |
| SMT's first results | EBITDA margin below its 14.1% IPO figure, or imports above 77% of purchases | Tests whether a stent maker deserves a premium multiple |
| Working capital | Poly debtor days above 103 or inventory days above 323 | Cash conversion decides whether profit becomes cash |
| US Section 232 | A determination that raises duties on medical goods | Poly's international share is 72% of consolidated sales |

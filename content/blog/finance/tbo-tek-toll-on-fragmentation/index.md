## Same bed, five times the price

**TBO Tek is valued at roughly the same dollar amount as Hotelbeds while earning about one-eighth of its EBITDA. The gap is not a mistake, but it is not paying for what most people think it is.**

In February 2025, HBX Group (Hotelbeds, the world's largest independent bed bank) listed in Madrid at EUR 11.50 a share, a EUR 2.84bn company. Twenty-one months later it trades near EUR 8, about EUR 1.98bn or roughly $2.2bn, and will earn around EUR 415m of adjusted EBITDA this fiscal year.

TBO Tek closed on 18 November at Rs 1,578, a market value of Rs 17,105 Cr. At Rs 94 to the dollar that is about **$1.8bn**. Its trailing twelve-month adjusted EBITDA is **Rs 528 Cr, about $56m**.

Roughly the same market value. One earns about eight times the other. Both sell, at first glance, the same thing: a hotel room, bought wholesale, sold to someone who is not the guest.

So either the Indian market is wrong by a factor of five, or TBO is not the same business. I went through the last six shareholder letters, the Q1 and Q2 FY27 calls, the September 2026 balance sheet, the Enforcement Directorate filings and the peer disclosures. My answer: **it is a different business, but the difference is not mainly software or India.**

A travel agent in Riyadh, Lagos or Lucknow does not pay TBO for the bed. Every wholesaler has the bed. The agent pays for what a search box cannot give:

- **Credit** in their own currency: book today, settle later.
- **Certainty**: someone fixes a failed booking at 2 a.m. in Dubai.
- **One ledger** for flights, hotels, transfers and visas, across 100+ countries.

TBO's toll is a **toll on fragmentation and trust**. That toll is real. But it is expensive to collect. Today TBO keeps about **1.2 paise of EBITDA per rupee of GTV**, against roughly 5 paise at Hotelbeds and 3 at WebBeds.

The share price assumes that collection cost falls sharply. Meanwhile AI pushes volume toward the one part of the business where TBO collects almost no toll. That tension is what this piece is about.

## One booking, followed

**On a Rs 1 lakh hotel booking, TBO hands more back to the agent than it keeps as profit.** The figures below are my illustration, built from TBO's reported organic Hotels + Ancillary ratios for Q1 and Q2 FY27 (Apr–Sep 2026).

An agent in Riyadh books a family of four into a Dubai hotel for four nights. The family pays the agent Rs 1,10,000. The agent pays TBO Rs 1,00,000. That Rs 1,00,000 is what TBO calls **GTV**. Here is where it goes.

| Who | Gets (Rs) | What for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hotel, or an upstream supplier | ~92,100 | The room, at a net rate |
| The agent (markup, paid by the family) | ~10,000 | The relationship, the advice, the visa, the WhatsApp at midnight |
| TBO, gross take ("revenue") | ~7,900 | Organic hotel take rate, ~7.9% of GTV |
| …handed back to the agent as incentives | ~2,400 | "Service fees": commissions shared to win the booking |
| …TBO gross profit | ~5,500 | Organic hotel GP, 5.5% of GTV in Q2 FY27 |
| …people, tech, payment gateways | ~2,800 | Organic SG&A ran at ~2.8% of GTV |
| …TBO EBITDA, if this were all hotels | ~2,700 | Before air dilutes it; blended, TBO keeps ~1,300 |

Three things stand out.

**The agent earns more than TBO.** The agent keeps ~Rs 10,000. TBO keeps Rs 1,300 to 2,700. Whoever owns the end customer owns most of the margin. TBO does not own the end customer and never will. That is the design, not a flaw.

**TBO pays its customers more than it earns.** In Q2 FY27 the organic business passed Rs 209 Cr back to agents as service fees and kept Rs 141 Cr as adjusted EBITDA. A tollbooth that refunds most of the toll to keep drivers on its road is collecting something real but thin.

**The time gap is the hidden product.** The agent often pays TBO weeks after booking, on a credit line. The hotel is often paid after check-out. TBO sits in the middle of that gap with other people's money. I come back to this in the float section, because it is where the moat and the risk both live.

## Two readings

**The market is paying for growth; the stock actually needs conversion.** Five pairs show where my reading departs.

| The market sees | I see |
| --- | --- |
| An Indian travel-tech compounder | A cross-border credit-and-service network run from India. India is ~36% of GTV but only ~11% of gross profit (Q1 FY27) |
| 60x P/E, ~30x EV/EBITDA, justified by 20%+ growth | ~30x for a business converting 1.2% of GTV into EBITDA, while peers converting 3–5% trade at 6–7x. The multiple is a bet on conversion, not growth |
| Classic Vacations lifts take rate from 6% to 8%+ | 12.4 of Classic's ~25 points of take are commissions passed straight to US advisors. On EBITDA per rupee of GTV, Classic matched organic at 1.3% in Q2 |
| AI threatens travel agents, so it threatens TBO | AI threatens the search layer. TBO's toll is on credit and servicing. The real risk is mix: AI agents buy through APIs, where TBO's toll is thinnest |
| Rs 2,060 Cr of cash, comfortably net cash | Mostly other people's money in transit. Trade payables Rs 6,067 Cr, receivables Rs 5,737 Cr, customer advances Rs 1,182 Cr. It is float, not surplus |

The mispricing is not in the level of the numbers. It is in **which numbers get disclosed**.

Every quarter TBO reports GTV, take rate, gross profit, monthly transacting buyers and regional growth. Those are the numbers the market prices.

What decides whether the toll is durable is never reported:

- What share of GTV is booked **on credit**, and what credit losses run at by agent cohort.
- How GTV splits between **agents on TBO's own platform and API buyers** (management said "about 50/50" on the Q2 call, once).
- How much hotel supply is **contracted directly** versus resold from other wholesalers.
- Whether agents who joined in 2023 still transact in 2026 (**cohort retention**).

The market prices what is disclosed. I am trying to price what is missing.

## Why the middleman exists

**A B2B travel wholesaler earns a toll in proportion to fragmentation on both sides of it. Where one side consolidates, the toll shrinks.** TBO's own history proves the rule.

Start from first principles. There are over a million hotels, most of them independent. There are hundreds of thousands of small travel agents in markets where families still buy trips offline: Saudi Arabia, the UAE, India, Nigeria, Indonesia, Brazil. A hotel in Istanbul cannot sign a contract with an agent in Jeddah, check his credit, collect his riyals and answer his calls in Arabic. Multiply that by every hotel and every agent and you get an N × M problem.

A wholesaler collapses N × M connections into N + M. That is the whole business. TBO says it serves agents in 100+ countries, in 11 languages, and collects payments in 55+ countries ([FY24 annual report](https://www.tbo.com/investors/Shareholder/Annual-Report.pdf)).

The value of that collapse depends on how fragmented each side stays. TBO's two products show it cleanly.

|  | Airlines | Hotels + Ancillary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supply side | Concentrated: a few carriers per route, moving to direct NDC distribution | Fragmented: a million mostly independent properties |
| Organic take rate, Q1 FY26 → Q1 FY27 | 2.6% → 2.2% (≈ 2.0% in Q2 FY27) | 8.4% → 7.9% |
| Gross profit per Rs 100 of GTV | ~Rs 1.0–1.2 | ~Rs 5.5 |
| Share of organic GTV, Q1 FY27 | 35% | 65% |

Where supply is concentrated, airlines keep the margin and cut incentives whenever they can. On the Q2 call management said air take fell to about 2.0% because of lower airline incentives and refund income. TBO passed more than half of that cut on to agents and still saw air gross profit dip to about 1.0% of GTV.

Where supply is fragmented, the wholesaler keeps far more. So TBO has spent five years moving from Indian air ticketing, where it started in 2006, to international hotels. Hotels went from 56.6% of GTV in Q1 FY25 to 67.5% in Q2 FY27 including Classic.

The demand side matters just as much. Big online travel agencies and tour operators are already served by Hotelbeds, Expedia and WebBeds through APIs. TBO's sweet spot is the **long tail**: small agents in emerging markets whom the big players find too costly, too risky and too foreign to serve.

That is the moat and the cost in one sentence. Serving the long tail needs key account managers on the ground, local payments, credit underwriting and multilingual support. That is why organic SG&A runs at about 2.8% of GTV, and why EBITDA is only about 1.1%.

One more first-principles point. **Agents multi-home.** A Dubai agent typically has logins on TBO, RateHawk, WebBeds and Expedia's agent site, and checks prices across them. The search is free to switch. What does not switch in a click is a credit line and a relationship manager who answers the phone. Hold onto that; it decides most of what follows.

## The fee stack

**TBO's take is not small. What it keeps is.** Of every Rs 100 of organic GTV, TBO books Rs 6 of revenue and ends with about Rs 1 of EBITDA.

The biggest single leak is the first one: incentives to agents, larger than the entire people bill. Those incentives are the price of being chosen by an agent who multi-homes. Payment gateways alone cost about 40% as much as EBITDA, a toll TBO pays to card networks and banks.

Peer multiples are my estimates from market caps in mid-November 2026.

Two honest caveats keep this from being a cheap shot. A third of TBO's GTV is air, which earns ~1% gross profit at anyone's hands. And both peers are cheap for their own reasons: HBX carries LBO-era debt and guided FY26 revenue growth of –4% to +1%; WebBeds fell 41% in one day in November 2025 on margin worries.

Still, the shape is clear. **TBO converts GTV into EBITDA at a quarter to a half of its peers' rate, and is priced at three to five times their multiple.** The only way both facts are right is if TBO's conversion rises a lot. If organic TBO converted at WebBeds' ~3%, trailing EBITDA would be roughly Rs 1,150 Cr instead of Rs 420 Cr. That gap is the entire bull case, and the rest of this piece asks whether TBO can close it.

## Who else sells the same bed

**In hotels alone, TBO is a mid-sized player in a market with at least four bigger or faster rivals, and the fastest one is attacking TBO's best region.** This is not a winner-take-all market, and nothing in the data says TBO is winning it.

| Player | Latest scale | Growth | Where it hurts TBO |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Expedia B2B | $11.2bn bookings in Q3 2026 alone (~$40bn+ a year) | +19% | Largest supply base; launching an MCP server for AI agents |
| HBX (Hotelbeds, Bedsonline) | EUR 8.2bn TTV, FY25 | +12% cc, but revenue –3% | Cutting price to buy growth: revenue falls while TTV rises |
| Emerging Travel Group (RateHawk) | $4.8bn GTV, 2025 | +30% | 110,000+ agents; GCC agents +12% to 9,000+, GCC bookings +66%; API bookings +78% in H1 2026 |
| WebBeds | A$5.8bn TTV, FY26 | +20% | Americas +41%; margins still rising |
| TBO Tek | Rs 42,637 Cr (~$4.5bn) TTM, of which hotels ~$3.0bn | Organic +16% cc | — |

Sources: [Expedia Q3 2026](https://finviz.com/news/415794/expedia-group-reports-third-quarter-2026-results), [HBX Q3 FY26](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/hbx-group-announces-third-quarter-080500049.html), [ETG 2025](https://www.traveldailynews.com/?p=448405), [RateHawk GCC](https://connectingtravel.affino.com/news/uae-based-ratehawk-expands-network-by-37), [RateHawk H1 2026](https://eglobaltravelmedia.com.au/2026/08/28/ratehawk-plugs-into-growth-as-api-bookings-rise/), [WebBeds FY26](https://www.webtravelgroup.com/news/fy26-results/).

The line that matters most is RateHawk. It is roughly TBO's size, private, growing about twice as fast, and built for the same customer: the small agent in an emerging source market. Its GCC growth lands squarely on the region TBO calls its highest-margin market.

TBO's own words confirm the pressure. The Q1 FY27 letter blamed the dip in organic hotel gross margin partly on "calibrated pricing to defend volume amid higher competition". On the Q2 call management said hotel margins should hold at Q2 levels, but "could fall if supply exceeds demand".

**Why several players survive.** Network effects here are weak on the demand side because agents multi-home. They are real on the supply side: more volume wins better net rates from hotels, which wins more agents. That pushes the industry toward consolidation, not monopoly.

History agrees. Hotelbeds bought GTA and Tourico. WebBeds rolled up JacTravel and Destinations of the World. The closest long-run analogy is the airline GDS (Amadeus, Sabre, Travelport): three survivors with durable fees because each owned a hard-to-replicate connection. Bed banks have never earned GDS-like returns, because their connection, a hotel contract, is easy to copy.

**What follows for TBO.** It can win only where the connection is hard to copy, which means the agent side: service density, local payments and credit in markets the others underserve. It cannot win on supply against Expedia. That is the lens for the business-by-business section.

## The float: a credit business wearing a tech badge

**TBO's entire net cash is other people's money.** At 30 September 2026, the operating float almost exactly equals net cash.

| Balance sheet, 30 Sep 2026 | Rs Cr |
| --- | --- |
| Trade payables (owed mostly to hotels and airlines) | 6,067 |
| + Contract liabilities (advances from customers) | 1,182 |
| – Trade receivables (owed by agents and buyers) | 5,737 |
| **= Operating float** | **1,512** |
| Cash, bank balances and liquid investments | 2,060 |
| – Borrowings (non-current 412 + current 154) | 566 |
| **= Net cash** | **1,494** |

Sources: [Q2 FY27 results, balance sheet extract](https://www.multibagg.ai/market-pulse/articles/tbo-tek-hotels-packages-growth-announcement-cmvzx69fr007t2vpez0ekhvwu), [Q2 FY27 summary](https://www.investywise.com/tbo-tek-limited-q2-fy27-financial-results-and-shareholder-letter). Payables exclude a small amount owed to micro and small enterprises.

Rs 1,512 Cr of float, Rs 1,494 Cr of net cash. That is not a coincidence. **There is no surplus cash to speak of.** If growth stalled and suppliers tightened terms, most of that cash would leave.

Now look at the size of the loan book. Receivables of Rs 5,737 Cr equal about **49 days of trailing GTV** and **3.3x shareholders' equity** of Rs 1,737 Cr. A 5% loss on those receivables, Rs 287 Cr, would erase a full year of profit after tax (Rs 273 Cr trailing). TBO is, functionally, a lender to tens of thousands of small travel agents across 100 countries, funded by hotels.

That is both the moat and the risk.

**Why credit is the moat.** An agent with a Rs 20 lakh credit line at TBO books there first, even when a rival is a little cheaper. Credit is the one thing that does not switch in a click. Co-founder Ankush Nijhawan said as much on the Q2 FY27 call: consumer OTAs could enter agent distribution, but channel conflict and "credit-line know-how" are the barriers. TBO's underwriting data, years of payment behaviour on tens of thousands of agents, is genuinely proprietary.

**Why credit is the risk.** Credit losses have looked small, but they come in lumps. TBO wrote off Rs 8.1 Cr of advances to the grounded airline Go First as an exceptional item. In Q2 FY27 it booked an Rs 8.1 Cr exceptional loss from suspected employee collusion and unauthorised bookings on a government travel agency account at its UAE subsidiary. In Q3 FY26 it restricted credit to a key North American API buyer, and that business shrank.

**Why cash flow is noisy.** Operating cash flow was Rs 237 Cr in FY23, Rs 226 Cr in FY24, Rs 289 Cr in FY25, then **minus Rs 21 Cr in FY26**, then **plus Rs 562 Cr in H1 FY27**. The H1 swing came from Rs 561 Cr more payables against Rs 369 Cr more receivables. Management credits collections of Middle East overdues and Brazil receivables, plus a shift from prepaid virtual cards to supplier credit lines. In the Q1 letter it said plainly that this release "is not a steady-state outcome".

So I treat TBO's cash flow over three-year windows, not quarters, and I treat the balance sheet as a credit book. The question an investor should ask every quarter is not "how much cash?" but "how fast are receivables growing relative to GTV, and what are the write-offs?" TBO does not answer the second part separately.

## Business by business: who collects the toll, who pays it

**TBO collects a durable toll in one place: hotels sold to small international agents on credit. Everything else is a funnel, a commodity, or a bet.** I sort each unit by what it owns, what it rents, and whether it collects a toll or pays one.

| Unit | Owns | Rents | Toll | Moat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **International agent platform, hotels** (Middle East, Europe, APAC, LatAm) | Agent relationships, the credit book, ~14,400 monthly transacting international agents, local payment rails, key account managers | Much of its hotel supply, cloud | Collects ~5.5% gross profit; pays ~2% back as agent incentives | **Partial, the strongest TBO has** |
| **API and enterprise distribution** (~50% of GTV per management) | Connectivity and uptime | Supply, and the customer relationship belongs to the buyer | Collects a thin margin; buyers compare suppliers every search | **Absent** |
| **India air** | The largest agent base: ~18,000 monthly transacting Indian agents | Airline inventory through GDS and airline direct connects | Take ~2.0% and falling; gross profit ~1% of GTV | **Absent** on economics; valuable only as the front door |
| **India hotels and outbound** | The same Indian agents, cross-sold | Hotel supply | Middling; India H&A GTV fell 2% in Q1 FY27, grew 12% in Q2 | **Partial**, more option than toll |
| **Classic Vacations** (US luxury wholesale, bought Oct 2025 for $125m) | Relationships with ~10,000 US travel advisors and the luxury consortia; deposit-funded negative working capital | Hotels, villas, ground services | Collects ~13% GP; pays ~12.4% of GTV to advisors; heavy call-centre costs | **Partial**, slow-growing |

The international organic business produced **84% of organic gross profit** in Q1 FY27 (Rs 325 Cr of Rs 385 Cr). India, the market people associate with TBO, produced the rest from roughly 40% of organic GTV.

**International agent platform.** This is the real company. International monthly transacting buyers hit a record 14,449 in Q2 FY27, up 17.4%. Europe grew 36% and APAC 33%. The Middle East grew 10% in constant currency through a regional war. Management notes that agents added in the same year contributed 6.9% of GTV in H1 FY26, versus 4.3% a year earlier, so the key-account hiring is converting. The moat is partial because RateHawk is building the same thing, and because nobody outside TBO knows how much of the supply is contracted directly.

**API distribution.** This half of GTV is where Expedia, Hotelbeds and RateHawk compete on price in every search. Management said on the Q2 call that take rate is lower in API and gross profit per booking is "marginally lower" too. I rate it absent: a buyer with an API connection to five wholesalers owes loyalty to none. It matters later, because AI agents will be API buyers.

**India air.** TBO's origin and still its largest buyer base, but the economics belong to airlines. When IndiGo or Air India trims incentives, TBO absorbs part of it and passes the rest to agents. Its value is strategic: agents arrive for air and stay for hotels. India hotels were about 20% of India GTV in Q1 FY26, which is the cross-sell runway.

**Classic Vacations.** TBO paid $125m for a business with trailing EBITDA of $11.5m, about 11x, on an acquisition case of 2.4% EBITDA per rupee of GTV. It is running at 2.5% on a trailing basis, so the purchase was not overpaid. But management itself calls Classic "a drag on growth" near term and an "investment and growth play, not a cost-efficiency play". TBO-sourced inventory is only about 8% of Classic's GTV. The TBO platform beta for Classic's advisors starts in late November 2026; management expects visible synergies "by next summer", Q2 FY28, if conditions normalise. Until then Classic is a bet on owning US luxury demand, not a toll.

## What is moving the wrong way

**The top of the funnel is eroding while the bottom improves.** Organic take rate slipped from 6.30% to 5.97% of GTV in six quarters. EBITDA per rupee of GTV only recently turned up, and part of that is the rupee.

{{< chart src="t02-organic-ratios.svg" label="Figure 2" source="TBO shareholder letters Q1 FY26–Q2 FY27: organic GTV, revenue, gross profit and adjusted EBITDA (Q1–Q2 FY26 pre-date Classic, so organic = consolidated)" >}}

Five things are moving against TBO, in order of how much they worry me.

1. **Take rate is drifting down.** Organic hotel take fell from 8.4% to 7.9% between Q1 FY26 and Q1 FY27. Management says it is supplier mix, more net-rate contracts and fewer commission ones, and gross profit held at 5.5–5.6% in Q2. That is a fair explanation. It also means TBO now passes fewer rupees through and keeps the same share, so growth must come from volume, not pricing.
2. **Airlines are taking back incentives.** Air take fell from 2.6% to about 2.0%. Air is a third of GTV. Management expects air gross profit to stay in a 1.0–1.2% band; it is at the bottom of that band now.
3. **India is flat.** Indian monthly transacting buyers were 18,486 in Q1 FY26 and 18,322 in Q1 FY27. On the Q2 call management said Indian buyers grew 1% in the quarter. India hotels fell 2% in Q1 as travellers traded down to cheaper destinations. The home market is not growing the buyer base.
4. **Operating leverage is two quarters old, and partly bought.** Organic EBITDA as a share of gross profit rose from 23.5% in Q4 FY26 to 32.1% in Q2 FY27. But management also said it had "paused new market development spending for now" and that Q3 margins will likely be lower than Q2. Leverage from cutting growth spend is a choice that can be reversed, not proof of structure.
5. **The rupee is flattering everything.** Q2 consolidated GTV grew 31% reported but 25% in constant currency; organic grew 22% reported, 16% constant. The rupee went from about 88.6 per dollar a year ago to about 94 now. TBO earns in dirhams, euros and dollars and spends largely in rupees, so a weak rupee lifts both growth and margins. It is a tailwind, not a moat, and it reverses.

The constant-currency picture is still decent: organic gross profit +14%, organic adjusted EBITDA +27% in Q2. But a business growing gross profit 14% in constant currency does not obviously deserve 60x trailing earnings. It deserves that only if the EBITDA line keeps bending up for years.

## AI, second order

**AI is not mainly a threat to TBO's customers. It is a threat to TBO's mix.** It pushes volume from the half of the business with a toll (agents on credit) toward the half without one (API buyers comparing prices).

Management's view, from the Q2 FY27 call: travel will be a major use case for AI agents. Simple point-to-point bookings are exposed; complex, multi-destination and luxury trips are much less so. TBO has launched experimental MCP integrations on Claude and ChatGPT and is using AI to raise sales-team productivity. That is a sensible first-order answer. The second-order effects matter more.

Start by decomposing what a small agent actually sells to a family:

| What the agent provides | Can an AI assistant replace it? |
| --- | --- |
| Search and price comparison | Yes, and better |
| Advice and itinerary building | Largely |
| Accountability: someone to call and blame | Weakly |
| Credit, cash, instalments, local payment | No |
| Visas, groups, Umrah, weddings, documents | Partly assisted, rarely replaced |
| Fixing disruptions on the ground | Partly |

The agents most at risk are those whose value is the first row: urban Indian air-ticketing desks. The least at risk are Gulf group and Umrah operators and US luxury advisors. TBO's mix is moving the right way here, toward international hotels and Classic.

**Second-order effect 1: fewer, bigger agents.** AI makes each agent more productive. A shop of three people can serve twice the clients. The number of agents may plateau while GTV per agent rises. TBO's favourite KPI, monthly transacting buyers, could stall even if GTV holds, and bigger agents negotiate harder on incentives. I would watch GTV per transacting buyer, not buyer count.

**Second-order effect 2: AI agents are API buyers.** When ChatGPT, Gemini or an OTA's assistant books a hotel, it needs wholesale inventory through an API. It routes on price and reliability. It needs no credit line, because the consumer pays by card. That is exactly the TBO unit I rated as having no moat. Expedia is launching an MCP server for its B2B partners ([Skift via Pulse, May 2026](https://www.pulse.bot/ai/news/expedia-to-launch-agentic-ai-tools-for-b2b-partners-d28d930e-dc52-4b05-833f-401e144c158d)). Hotel-tech vendors are wiring hotels straight into AI assistants ([Hospitality Net](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4132086/agentic-hospitalitys-travelos-mcp-chatgpt-app-is-putting-brands-not-intermediaries-at-the-center-of-ai-bookings)). AI volume will grow, but into TBO's thinnest margin.

**Second-order effect 3: the scarce layer moves to credit and settlement.** If search is free, what stays scarce is trust: underwriting a Lagos agent, settling in 55 currencies, absorbing a no-show. That is TBO's best asset. The upside option is to become the credit and settlement layer for bookings in markets where cards are scarce, for human agents and AI agents alike. Nothing in the disclosures suggests TBO is pricing that layer explicitly yet.

**Second-order effect 4: AI cuts everyone's cost to serve the long tail.** This is TBO's biggest margin lever: support, sales and servicing are most of SG&A. But the same tools cut RateHawk's cost of entering TBO's markets. A moat built on being cheapest to serve small agents erodes when serving small agents gets cheap for everyone. What does not erode is proprietary credit history.

Net: AI lowers TBO's take and lowers its costs. Which falls faster decides EBITDA per rupee of GTV, and nobody can know that today. A 30x multiple quietly assumes the friendly answer.

## How the reported numbers mislead

**Revenue growth overstates TBO's progress; profit after tax understates it. Gross profit and constant-currency organic EBITDA are the honest lines.** Six adjustments explain the gap.

1. **Classic is booked gross.** Classic's ~25% take rate includes a 12.38% commission passed straight through to US travel advisors. That is why Q2 consolidated revenue grew 53% while GTV grew 31%, and organic revenue only 14%. Revenue is the wrong denominator for TBO now; use gross profit, which strips pass-throughs.
2. **Two revenue clocks.** TBO's organic hotels recognise revenue at booking; Classic recognises at check-in. Classic's adjusted EBITDA was 3.4% of GTV in Q1 FY27 (peak US travel) and 1.3% in Q2 (its weakest quarter). Quarter-on-quarter comparisons of the consolidated business are mostly noise for the next year.
3. **"Adjusted" leaves out real costs.** Adjusted EBITDA excludes ESOP expense (about Rs 6 Cr a quarter) and acquisition costs. Small, but recurring for ESOPs.
4. **Acquisition costs move below the line.** Depreciation and amortisation rose from Rs 16 Cr in Q2 FY26 to Rs 34 Cr in Q2 FY27, including provisional purchase-price amortisation of about $0.83m a quarter on Classic. Finance costs rose from Rs 9 Cr to Rs 17 Cr on the acquisition loan ($60m still outstanding after a $10m prepayment). That is most of why PAT grew 13% while adjusted EBITDA grew 47%.
5. **The rupee sits on both sides.** It lifts reported growth (about 6 points in Q2) and then shows up again as FX losses below EBITDA: Rs 8.9 Cr in Q2, with about 75% of open exposure hedged.
6. **Items not yet in the numbers.** The Rs 22.2 Cr FEMA penalty on the company (plus Rs 2.47 Cr on each joint MD) is not provided for; the auditor added an emphasis of matter. The Rs 8.1 Cr UAE loss is booked as exceptional, but the investigation is open.

My normalised earnings: trailing PAT of Rs 273 Cr, plus roughly Rs 25 Cr of post-tax purchase-price amortisation, plus the Rs 8 Cr one-off, gives about **Rs 305 Cr**. That puts the stock at **~56x normalised trailing earnings**, against 63x reported. Better, not cheap.

The cleaner lens is enterprise value over EBITDA, with one twist from the float section. If you net off all Rs 2,060 Cr of cash, EV/EBITDA is about **30x**. If you accept that the cash is float and ignore it, it is about **33x**. I use 30x as the generous case.

## Governance and red flags

**None of the red flags is large in rupees. Two of them point at the same weak spot: the payment and credit plumbing that is also TBO's moat.**

**The FEMA order.** The Enforcement Directorate searched a TBO office in Gurugram on 13 May 2022. The allegation: TBO let foreign travel agents book airline tickets and accepted rupee payments from parties other than those receiving the service. The complaint cited Rs 49.37 Cr; TBO puts total alleged contraventions at Rs 71.2 Cr. TBO tried to compound the matter; the RBI's foreign exchange department declined post-facto approval in April and September 2025. On 16 October 2026 the ED imposed Rs 22.2 Cr on the company and Rs 2.47 Cr on each joint MD. TBO will appeal and has made no provision ([FEMA filing summary](https://www.multibagg.ai/market-pulse/articles/tbo-tek-fema-penalty-appeal-announcement-cmvzx19in007r2vpeabgecp1b)).

Separately, a money-laundering chargesheet in a Special CBI court in Kolkata concerns third parties who used the TBO portal. TBO's lawyers say neither the company nor its people are charged.

The money is about 8% of a year's profit. The signal is bigger: a cross-border booking platform that extends credit and accepts third-party payments is an attractive pipe for moving value. Agent onboarding and payment checks are a regulatory surface, not just an ops detail.

**The UAE loss.** After Q2 FY27, TBO found suspected collusion among some employees of its Dubai subsidiary, producing unauthorised bookings on a government travel agency's account. Loss booked: Rs 8.1 Cr, with an external investigation, legal action and insurance claims under way. The CFO said nothing similar had happened in five to seven years. Fine, but it is exactly the kind of failure a credit-and-settlement business must not repeat.

**Supply overhang.** Pre-IPO investors still hold about a fifth of the company: General Atlantic 14.4%, Augusta TBO 3.5%, TBO Korea Holdings ~2.9%. Augusta and TBO Korea sold 6.8% in a March 2025 block at about Rs 1,200; Augusta sold another 2% in July 2026 at about Rs 1,500 ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/buzz/two-overseas-entities-to-sell-stake-in-tbo-tek-in-inr-400-cr-block-deals/), [Entrackr](https://entrackr.com/news/augusta-tbo-offloads-2-stake-in-tbo-tek-for-rs-327-cr-12161055)). General Atlantic's stake is worth about Rs 2,460 Cr at today's price; expect it to come to market in blocks.

**Who owns it now.** In the September 2026 quarter, foreign investors fell from 28.9% to 22.5% while mutual funds rose from 18.3% to 24.2% ([Trendlyne](https://trendlyne.com/equity/2245414/TBOTEK/tbo-tek-ltd/)). Domestic funds are absorbing foreign selling, which supports the multiple while SIP flows last.

**What is reassuring.** The founders have not sold a share since listing. Promoter holding is 44.4%, unpledged, split between Ankush Nijhawan's LAP Travel (21.6%), Gaurav Bhatnagar (17.3%) and Manish Dhingra (4.9%). Disclosure quality is above the Indian mid-cap norm: organic and acquired numbers are split, constant-currency growth is given, and investor questions are answered in writing every quarter.

## Valuation: what the price already assumes

**At Rs 1,578, the market needs TBO to convert about 1.75% of GTV into EBITDA by FY30 while growing 16% a year, and still be valued at 20x then.** Today it converts 1.24%.

The multiples, all on trailing numbers to September 2026:

| Measure | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Market value | Rs 17,105 Cr (~10.84 Cr shares at Rs 1,578) |
| P/E, reported (TTM PAT Rs 273 Cr) | ~63x |
| P/E, normalised (~Rs 305 Cr) | ~56x |
| EV / adjusted EBITDA (TTM Rs 528 Cr) | ~30x, or ~33x treating cash as float |
| Market value / gross profit (TTM Rs 2,091 Cr) | ~8x |
| Price / FY28E EPS (Motilal Oswal ~Rs 50) | ~31x |

**The brokers' re-rating is in the multiple, not the earnings.** Motilal Oswal initiated in March 2026 at a Rs 1,360 target, 28x an FY28 EPS of Rs 48.7. Its latest target is Rs 1,912, at 38x FY28E. The FY28 EPS behind it moved from Rs 48.7 to roughly Rs 50 ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/markets/news/motilal-oswal-initiates-coverage-on-tbo-tek-with-buy-sees-28-upside-126032500260_1.html), [Groww](https://groww.in/stocks/tbo-tek-ltd)). Eight months, a 3% change in the earnings view, a 36% change in the multiple.

Now reverse-engineer the price, the same way one asks what market share a valuation implies. Assume a 12% cost of equity. Today's Rs 17,105 Cr must grow to about Rs 25,150 Cr by FY30 (Apr 2029–Mar 2030). I treat net cash as float and ignore it. What EBITDA per rupee of GTV does that require?

| FY30 EBITDA ÷ GTV needed | Exit at 15x EBITDA | Exit at 20x | Exit at 25x |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GTV grows 12% a year (to ~Rs 63,400 Cr) | 2.64% | 1.98% | 1.59% |
| GTV grows 16% a year (to ~Rs 71,700 Cr) | 2.34% | **1.75%** | 1.40% |
| GTV grows 20% a year (to ~Rs 80,700 Cr) | 2.08% | 1.56% | 1.25% |

Read it this way. If TBO grows GTV 16% a year, roughly its current constant-currency organic rate plus Classic, it must lift conversion from 1.24% to 1.75% **and** still command 20x EBITDA in 2030, just to earn a 12% return from here. Merely holding 1.24% requires 20% growth and a 25x exit.

Is 1.75% achievable? It is plausible, not assured. The organic business hit 1.30% in Q2 FY27, its best quarter, helped by the rupee and paused spending. Hotels are ~two-thirds of GTV and convert well above the blend; air drags it down. If hotels reach three-quarters of GTV and SG&A grows at half the rate of gross profit, the blended number gets to about 1.6–1.8% by FY30. That is the base case below.

The point is not that the stock is expensive in an absolute sense. It is that **the price already discounts the base case**. The return from here depends on beating it.

## Three futures

**My base case is worth about today's price; the spread between bear and bull is roughly –50% to +55%.** All three are rough, valued at FY29 (Apr 2028–Mar 2029) and discounted back at 12%.

|  | Bed bank | Toll road | Credit network |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Story | RateHawk and API pricing cap margins; AI shifts mix to API; conversion stalls | Classic synergies land, hotels keep gaining share, SG&A grows at half the pace of GP | Classic plus TBO supply wins US luxury; credit and settlement become a priced product; ME recovers fully |
| GTV growth a year | 10% | 16% | 21% |
| FY29 GTV | ~Rs 54,100 Cr | ~Rs 61,800 Cr | ~Rs 68,700 Cr |
| EBITDA ÷ GTV | 1.15% | 1.50% | 1.90% |
| FY29 EBITDA | ~Rs 620 Cr | ~Rs 930 Cr | ~Rs 1,300 Cr |
| EV ÷ EBITDA at FY29 | 15x (still double peers) | 22x | 25x |
| Value per share at FY29 | ~Rs 1,000 | ~Rs 2,050 | ~Rs 3,190 |
| **Worth today, at 12%** | **~Rs 760 (–52%)** | **~Rs 1,560 (–1%)** | **~Rs 2,430 (+54%)** |
| My odds | 30% | 45% | 25% |

Weighting those odds gives about Rs 1,540, within a few percent of the current price. On these numbers the stock is **fairly priced for a good outcome**, with no margin of safety. I would want either a price nearer Rs 1,150–1,250 (a 20–25% cushion against the base case) or evidence from the kill switches below that the bull case is gaining ground.

What moves you between columns is almost entirely one ratio: **EBITDA per rupee of GTV.** Growth matters, but every 0.1 point of conversion on FY29's base-case GTV is worth about Rs 62 Cr of EBITDA, or roughly Rs 125 a share at 22x. Growth of one extra point a year is worth far less. That is why the kill switches focus on conversion, credit and mix, not on headline GTV.

## The bear case, and the tests that would settle it

**The strongest bear case: TBO is a bed bank with an emerging-market credit book, priced like a software platform.** I take it seriously, so I set dated tests that would prove it right or wrong.

The bear argument, at full strength:

- Agents multi-home, so there is no demand-side network effect. Supply is the same everywhere. That leaves service and credit, both of which get cheaper to copy with AI.
- The fastest-growing rival, RateHawk, is private, roughly TBO's size, and growing about twice as fast in TBO's best region.
- The recent operating leverage came partly from pausing growth spend and partly from a weak rupee. Neither is structural.
- Half of GTV is API business with no moat, and AI-agent volume will land there.
- The loan book is 3.3x equity, and losses arrive in lumps: Go First, a UAE fraud, a cut-off North American buyer.
- A fifth of the equity is held by financial investors who have been selling.

The bull answer: TBO's founders took sales from Rs 571 Cr in FY20 to Rs 3,390 Cr TTM through a pandemic and a war. International transacting buyers are growing 17%. Classic is running on its acquisition case. And disclosure is good enough to check all of this every quarter.

Here is how I will check it. TBO reports roughly in early February (Q3), late May (Q4), late July (Q1) and early November (Q2).

| Test | Pass | Fail = thesis broken | Read on | Now |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Conversion:** organic adj. EBITDA ÷ GTV, trailing 12 months | ≥ 1.20% | ≤ 1.05% | Q2 FY28 results, Nov 2027 | 1.09% |
| **2. Pricing power:** organic hotel GP ÷ GTV | ≥ 5.3% each quarter | < 5.0% for two quarters running | Every quarter through FY28 | 5.5% |
| **3. Demand moat:** international organic transacting buyers, YoY | ≥ 12% | < 5% for two quarters running | Every quarter through FY28 | +17.4% |
| **4. Classic:** trailing adj. EBITDA ÷ GTV; TBO-sourced share of Classic GTV | ≥ 2.4% and ≥ 15% | < 2.0% | Q2 FY28, Nov 2027 | 2.5% and ~8% |
| **5. Credit:** receivables growth vs GTV growth (YoY, half-yearly); credit or fraud exceptionals | Within 5 points; none > Rs 10 Cr | Either breached | H2 FY27 (May 2027) and H1 FY28 (Nov 2027) | Rs 8.1 Cr in Q2 FY27 |
| **6. Regulation:** FEMA appeal; any PMLA proceeding | Appeal admitted; company not charged | Company or a joint MD charged | Ongoing | Appeal planned |
| **7. Disclosure:** API vs agent-platform GTV split and share booked on credit | Reported in FY27 annual report | Still undisclosed | Annual report, ~Aug 2027 | Verbal "50/50" only |

Three of these are formal kill switches for me: **test 1 failing** (conversion stuck), **test 2 failing** (a price war has started), and **test 5 or 6 failing** (the credit and payment plumbing is weaker than it looks). Any one of those turns the base case into the bed-bank case.

One more watch item, not a kill switch: any acquisition above $100m, or any share-funded deal, before Classic's synergies show up in Q2 FY28.

## What TBO teaches beyond TBO

**Five lessons travel well to any marketplace or distributor.**

1. **A middleman's toll tracks fragmentation on both sides.** Watch which side is consolidating. Airlines consolidated, and TBO's air take fell from 2.6% to 2.0% in a year. Hotels have not, and hotel take holds near 8%.
2. **Compare EBITDA per unit of transaction value, not revenue.** Revenue multiples break when pass-throughs differ, as Classic's 12.4% advisor commissions show. EBITDA per rupee of GTV is the one ratio that compares TBO, WebBeds and Hotelbeds fairly.
3. **In B2B distribution, the moat is often the balance sheet.** Read receivables the way a lender would. Ask what share of volume is on credit and what the losses are by cohort.
4. **Net cash in a float business is not surplus.** If float roughly equals net cash, as it does at TBO, there is nothing spare to count.
5. **When a re-rating comes from the multiple rather than the earnings, the stock is a bet on flows.** TBO's targets rose 36% on a 3% change in FY28 earnings, while domestic funds bought what foreign funds sold.

And one on AI: **ask which layer becomes scarce.** For intermediaries, AI moves value from search toward trust, credit and settlement. Mix shifts will matter more than headcount savings.
